# Common Knowledge

*What follows is not lore you had to study. It is what your character knows in their bones — the things no one in the Sepia Uplands explains because everyone already understands them. Arriving at the table ignorant of these facts is the equivalent of not knowing what rain is.*

---

## The World Is Old and Most of It Is Broken

The Flanaess is built on ruins. There are structures in the Sepia Uplands older than the oldest human settlement in the region. Nobody built them recently. Nobody knows who built them. The appropriate response when you find one is caution, not excitement — what was sealed was sealed for a reason, and whatever reason it was, it was probably sufficient.

The standing stones on the high moor predate the Great Migrations. The Flan who still walk these lands call them the Old Bones. They don't touch them during festival weeks. You probably shouldn't touch them at all without consulting someone who knows what they're looking at.

---

## Magic Is Rare and People Fear It

Most common folk in the Sepia Uplands have never seen a casting. They know what magic *is* — they've heard stories — but a fireball or a summoning is as real to them as a siege engine: theoretically possible, practically terrifying, and not something you deploy in a village without consequences.

Displaying flashy magic in a settlement without warning will frighten people. It may provoke hostility. It will certainly be remembered and discussed for years. This is not a restriction on what you can do. It is information about what it costs.

Healing magic is a partial exception — practical benefit tends to override theological anxiety. But even a healer should be matter-of-fact about it, not theatrical.

---

## The Quag Mires Are Not Survivable Alone

The bog system north of Ritterstadt's palisade produces undead. Not metaphorically. Not as a rumor. The druids at Ehlonna's Mount track the numbers. In a Blackfrost Bloom year — which this is — the margin between "expedition territory" and "where the bog-drowned walk" moves south. Students who wander north alone do not return. This is a documented pattern, not a hypothetical.

The Blackfrost Bloom is currently active. You know this because the ground is colder than it should be in Planting, ice forms on still water at night, and there are small translucent crystals on north-facing stone that were not there last year. The druids call it a wound in the world bleeding cold. Whether that is literally true or poetically true, the effect is the same.

## There Is No Law Here

Perrenland's tax collectors stop at Traft, four days south. Beyond that, there is no canton court, no garrison to summon, and no official authority to appeal to. Ritterstadt's Ritter — Konrad von Eisfeld — has the right of a local lord in the old Oeridian tradition, but his jurisdiction ends at the palisade and is enforced by eleven farmsteads and a guardhouse with eight soldiers. Everything beyond that is settled by the people involved, or not settled at all.

The University provides a framework for student conduct. The Ritter provides a framework for village governance. Neither has teeth in the wilderness. Plan accordingly.

---

## The Wolf Nomads Are Not Bandits

The Wolf Nomad outriders who watch Ritterstadt from the high moor are not random raiders. They are Flan-descended nomadic herders following ancestral migration routes that predate this settlement by centuries. The standing stones on the moor are sacred to them. The Sepia Uplands, by their reckoning, are their land.

They have not attacked Ritterstadt. This is a deliberate choice on their part. Treating them as generic "monsters" or "enemies" would be an error — politically, practically, and for reasons the University's faculty would describe as "long-term strategic foolishness." An encounter with a Wolf Nomad outrider is a diplomatic situation. It should be treated as one.

---

## The Dwarves Are Autonomous and Remember Everything

Clan Irongrip holds Bitter Pass. Clan Brock holds the western approaches. Both clans have been in the Clatspurs for over four centuries. They trade in metal and information. They do not explain their politics to outsiders. They have opinions — well-formed, deeply-held opinions — about every significant event in the region going back further than any human institution's records.

Hogar Irongrip, who operates the smithy in Ritterstadt, is a Clan Irongrip exile. The specific circumstances of his exile are not discussed. Do not ask about Bitter Pass politics in his presence unless you are prepared for a silence that will last until you change the subject.

## Ritterstadt's Rules Are Unwritten but Real

Some things every person in the hamlet understands:

**You work.** There is no population surplus. If you are present, you are expected to contribute — to watches, to emergency labor, to basic communal maintenance. The Ritter does not post a list of chores. He notices who helps and who doesn't.

**You do not endanger the hamlet.** This means you don't lead something back to the palisade. You don't start conflicts that the garrison has to finish. You don't make promises to factions that put Ritterstadt in the middle.

**You pay your debts.** In the barter economy of the farmsteads, a debt unpaid is a social wound that festers. University credits are separate from this system, but the principle applies: if someone helps you, you owe them something, and the hamlet keeps track.

**The Wachturm is off-limits.** The old tower on the northern escarpment is not a University building and is not a landmark to investigate on your first week. There are reasons for this. The reasons have been sufficient thus far.

## Winter Kills

Every winter on the northern frontier, people die. They die from cold, from starvation if the harvest was poor, from illness, from bad decisions in bad weather. The autumn slaughter at the Big Barn is not a celebration of abundance — it is a calculation about how many animals the hamlet can feed through the snow. What doesn't make the count gets slaughtered, salted, and eaten. The people who survive Sunsebb and Fireseek without significant losses consider themselves fortunate, because they are.

This shapes how people think, plan, and judge risk. When a villager looks at your plan and shakes their head, they are usually doing math you haven't done yet.
